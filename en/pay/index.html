Career · Pay

Wind turbine technician pay

Most people who search for this page are looking for a single number. I am not going to give one — and if you read to the end you will agree with me. What I will do instead is teach you to read an offer: two technicians doing the same job end up in very different places, and the one who knows where that gap comes from stands somewhere else in a negotiation.

The short answer

A wind turbine technician’s pay is not one number but the result of six variables: whether you work for the manufacturer or a contractor, whether you can do mechanical and electrical work together, site allowance and shift pattern, overseas per diem, your certification level, and your field experience.

When you weigh up an offer, look at the whole package, not the base salary, and ask this one question: “Is this figure gross, does it include the site allowance, and who pays for accommodation and travel?” If the answer is not clear, be careful.

Put your own figures into the offer reader below; it shows how much of the package is fixed and how much stops the day the project ends.

Section 01

Why there is no number here

The easy way to write this page would have been a table: “starting pay is this, after five years it is that.” It would have brought traffic too. I am not doing it, for three reasons:

  • Numbers go stale fast. Pay is revised more than once a year in many markets. A figure I write today will point you the wrong way in six months — and you will walk into a negotiation believing it is current.
  • There is no such thing as the average. Two people on the same site, on the same turbine, doing the same job can be on noticeably different packages. Quoting an average hides that the gap exists — when the gap is exactly what you need to understand.
  • Base salary misleads. An offer of “45” can be worse than one of “38 plus the full package” if accommodation and travel are on you and there is no site allowance. A single number cannot carry that difference.

The damage I see most in this industry is exactly this: people who take a figure they read online as their reference and then turn down a good offer, or accept a bad one. Instead of a number, let me give you the method for reading one.

Section 02

The six levers that set your pay

  • 01 · Manufacturer or contractor. The turbine manufacturer’s own service organisation and the contractor serving the wind farm owner pay very differently for the same work. The manufacturer side is usually more corporate and more settled; the contractor side gets you work faster. You cannot be choosy in your first job. Be choosy in your second.
  • 02 · Both trades. This is the biggest lever. There are few technicians who can do mechanical and electrical work, and that is the person wanted when a crew is put together. Stay on one side and the number of people who can replace you goes up, so your bargaining power goes down.
  • 03 · Site allowance and shift. Base salary alone misleads. Work it out together with travel, accommodation, meals, site allowance and shift premium. In some offers a third of the package comes from these — and they can stop when the project does.
  • 04 · Overseas projects. On European installation projects the daily per diem changes the picture completely. That was my own period on Danish and other European sites. If your passport is ready, this door is open.
  • 05 · Certification level. A technician with BST plus BTT plus manufacturer training is not on the same money as someone with BST alone. Specialist modules such as high voltage, blade repair or advanced rescue feed straight into the package.
  • 06 · Experience and brand variety. A technician who has worked on three brands finds work more easily than someone who spent ten years on one. Variety helps more than seniority here.

Section 04

Five sentences to say at interview

Asking these five introduces the company to you as much as the other way round. How clear the answers are tells you more about working there than the salary does.

  1. “Is this figure gross or net?” The most basic question and, surprisingly, the one most often skipped. Ask for the answer in writing.
  2. “Is the site allowance included in this figure, or on top?” If it is included, your pay drops in any month you are not on site. You need to know that up front.
  3. “Who pays for accommodation, travel and meals?” If all three are on you, there is a serious gap between the apparent value of the offer and its real value. The calculator above shows it.
  4. “Who pays for GWO training and the refresher?” The industry standard is that the employer covers it. If they do not, that is information about the company. Detail on the GWO page.
  5. “Which training will I get in the first year?” As important as the salary. A company that trains you makes you a more expensive technician in two years; a company that does not keeps you where you are.
Section 05

How it moves year by year

The way to describe progression without giving numbers is to say what changes at each threshold. These are the points where pay jumps:

  • 0–1 year — entry. Cleaning, lubrication, bolt checks, moving material. Your bargaining power is close to nil; in this period negotiate training, not money.
  • 1–2 years — independent maintenance tasks. You take jobs on your own during scheduled maintenance. The first real rise comes at this threshold; if it does not, ask why.
  • 2–4 years — choosing a speciality. Electrical faults, major components, blades, commissioning or site supervision. The biggest jump is here, because the number of people who can replace you is now falling. This is the road to working both trades.
  • 4–7 years — crew responsibility and going abroad. Crew leader, commissioning lead, or overseas installation projects. The per diem period usually falls here and it changes the picture.
  • 7 years and beyond — site management, training, consulting. The threshold where you leave the tower base and start selling the knowledge. It is also where the degree gets asked about; the moment a diploma pays off is not your first job, it is this one.
Section 06

Working abroad and per diem

The biggest jump in earnings in this industry is on overseas installation projects. Let me describe it without any romance.

  • Per diem is added to the base salary, it does not replace it. If the offer says “so much per day”, ask how many days a month you will actually be on site. The difference between twenty days and ten changes the whole offer.
  • It stops when the project does. Treating a per diem period as permanent income and taking on commitments accordingly is the most common mistake I have seen in this industry.
  • Preconditions: a passport ready to go, and any national service obligations completed. Offers usually require you to travel at short notice; get the passport now.
  • Ask about tax and social security. Be clear about which country you will be insured in and whether your contributions at home keep running.
  • The return is not only money. Having worked on a European project becomes the line people ask about most on your CV afterwards.
Section 07

Red flags

Slow down if you see these in an offer.

  • No straight answer to “gross or net”. That is a sign of something you will be arguing about later.
  • No written offer. A verbal figure can come back as a “misunderstanding” after you have started.
  • You pay for the GWO training and it is tied into the contract. If a minimum service period is required in return, read the period and the penalty clause.
  • The site allowance makes up most of the package. The calculator above shows this: if the fixed part is small, a month without site work leaves you in trouble.
  • Crew size is far too small for the number of turbines. Regardless of pay, that means burnout. Ask at interview: “how many people to how many turbines?”
  • Nobody tracks your certificate validity. A company that lets your refresher slip will one day be unable to put you on site at all.
Section 08

Frequently asked questions

How much does a wind turbine technician earn?
There is no single figure, and most of the numbers you find online are copied from one another. Six things set the pay: manufacturer or contractor, whether you can do mechanical and electrical work together, site allowance and shift, overseas per diem, certification level and experience. Read an offer from the whole package, not the base salary.
Which matters more, base salary or total package?
The total package, but together with its split. If most of the package comes from site allowance and per diem, a month without site work drops your income noticeably. Always know what the fixed part is.
Can a new technician negotiate?
Money negotiation is weak in a first job; negotiate training instead. The question “which training will I get in the first year” affects your salary two years from now more than haggling over the base ever will.
What is the single best thing to learn for pay?
Being able to do mechanical and electrical work together. Few technicians in the field can do both; that is the person wanted when a crew is formed, and as the pool of people who can replace you shrinks, your bargaining power grows.
Do overseas projects really pay better?
Yes, the daily per diem changes the picture completely. But per diem stops when the project ends; do not mistake it for permanent income and take on long-term commitments. A ready passport is the precondition.
How do I compare two offers?
Put the items of each offer separately into the offer reader on this page. Once you line up the real monthly value, the annual total and the fixed-to-conditional split, which offer is better often changes.
