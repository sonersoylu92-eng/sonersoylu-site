Model guide

Vestas V110/3.0M

3.0 MW rated power, 110 metre rotor. A workhorse of the mid-to-late 2000s and still running in numbers across Türkiye. The generation before the V126, and the last of the Vestas 3 MW machines built around a parallel-axis gearbox.

Model-specific systems

Parallel-axis gearbox

  • Layout: successive parallel shaft stages to an overall ratio near 1:81.
  • Strengths: mechanically simple and forgiving; stress is spread across conventional meshes rather than concentrated as in a planetary set.
  • Costs: heavier and noisier than a planetary box of the same rating.
  • Oil: 700–750 L, nominal 45–65 °C, alarm above roughly 75 °C.

Pitch and yaw

  • Pitch motors: 15–18 kW, maximum rate around 5–7 °/s — slower than the V126.
  • Yaw motors: 7–10 kW; slewing bearing about 3500 mm.
  • Alignment tolerance: ±3–5°, wider than the ±1° of later machines, with less vane sensor redundancy.

Assembly stages (16–17 days)

  1. Days 1–2: site preparation, crane pad and tooling checks, safety briefing.
  2. Days 3–8: sectional tower erection to 80–120 m.
  3. Days 9–10: nacelle lift, 550–600 t. Gearbox alignment is more sensitive here than on planetary machines.
  4. Days 11–14: blade installation, 53.5 m blades at roughly 20 t each, root flange bolting.
  5. Days 15–16: electrical and cooling — converter cabling, coolant fill and bleed, SCADA.
  6. Day 17: commissioning — grid synchronisation, pitch calibration, performance test.

Faults seen most

  • Shaft seal oil leakage: the defining V110 issue, typically appearing after 8–12 years and developing slowly enough to pass unnoticed. Mechanism worked through in Case 02.
  • Gear pitting: from sustained overload combined with poor oil circulation.
  • Converter thermal problems: including the intermittent, power-dependent kind described in Case 05, which was found on a V110.
  • Pitch asymmetry: with less sensor redundancy than later machines, a single failed sensor has more effect.
  • Yaw bearing drag: grease ageing raises preload and drives yaw motor current up.

Field note

The V110 is a dependable machine, and its weak point is predictable enough to plan around. Around the ten-year mark the shaft seals start to pass oil, and because the loss is slow nobody notices until the temperature has moved. Operators who run metal analysis on schedule catch it early; operators who only top up the oil find out later and more expensively.

Blade vibration deserves more attention on this model than on the V90 — 53.5 m blades are long enough for resonance to matter — so periodic borescope and vibration checks earn their place.
