From the field · Case 04

Generator Temperature Alarm — Is the Fault in the Sensor?

Vestas V126. When the generator winding temperature reaches 116 °C the turbine stops on the alarm, comes back after a reset, and stops again the same day. Yet the rest of the machine looks healthy: normal sound, no vibration, cooling running. When the reading and the machine's behaviour disagree, one of them is wrong — and it is worth finding out which before you touch the generator.

Symptom

  • Alarm: Generator temperature high, 116 °C (threshold 115 °C). The turbine stops.
  • After a reset: the turbine comes back normally; the alarm repeats 2–3 times a day in the same wind.
  • Timing: the alarms come in the afternoon, at rated power (13–15 m/s wind).
  • Trend: over the last three days the peak reading went 110 → 112 → 116 °C — a tidy climb of about 2 °C per day.
  • Everything else: power, current and speed normal. No unusual sound, vibration or smell.

First assessment

A generator genuinely overheating does not stay quiet. Its sound changes, it smells, and the cooling circuit shows the load somewhere. None of that was present. A severe number with none of its consequences is the classic shape of an instrumentation fault rather than a machine fault.

The other clue was the trend. A real thermal problem tracks load and weather; it does not climb a tidy 2 °C every day regardless. A steady, load-independent drift points to something moving in the measurement chain, not to the machine heating up.

Step-by-step diagnosis

  1. Reviewed the SCADA log together with the load data. At the alarm moments power, current and speed were at rated values; the generator was not overloaded. The temperature rose day by day, not with load.
  2. Stopped the turbine and took a physical reading with an IR thermometer. This is the step that decides the case. The stopped generator's housing measured about 62 °C while SCADA showed 103 °C at the same moment — a gap of 41 °C. A sensor inside the winding reads somewhat hotter than the housing surface; a few degrees is normal, 41 is not.
  3. Took several readings around the housing. To be sure the IR reading was not a local artefact. The distribution was even; there was no local hot spot.
  4. Checked the cooling circuit. Fan running (3.2 A), airflow normal, filter clean. Nothing to explain a real 116 °C, which reinforced the instrumentation hypothesis.
  5. Measured the sensor resistance. A PT100 is 100 Ω at 0 °C and rises by roughly 0.385 Ω per degree. For the measured 62 °C the expected value is about 124 Ω. The sensor read about 140 Ω, which corresponds to roughly 103 °C in the PT100 table. SCADA was converting the resistance correctly; the resistance itself was wrong.
  6. Checked the wiring and terminals. A loose or corroded terminal adds series resistance and produces exactly this picture. The connections were sound, so the deviation was in the sensor element itself.
  7. Reviewed the sensor's service age. It had been in service for about four years.

Chain of evidence: a load-independent, steadily climbing temperature → a 41 °C gap between IR and SCADA → nothing wrong with cooling → the PT100 resistance matches the SCADA temperature, not the real one → connections sound. Each step confirms the one before.

Root cause

Drift in the winding temperature sensor (PT100). The element showed about 16 Ω too much resistance, which lifted the reading by roughly 41 °C. SCADA faithfully converted that wrong resistance into a temperature, and the control system did exactly what it was designed to do — protect the machine — by stopping a generator that was in good health.

The cost of this fault was not damage. It was lost production, plus the risk of someone opening up a healthy generator chasing a number.

Fix

  1. Temporary correction: a correction based on the physically verified gap was entered in SCADA and the turbine returned to production. This is done under the site's and the manufacturer's procedure, with authorisation, documented and with an end date. While it is in place the reading is compared with an IR measurement regularly; if the drift continues, the gap changes. If the generator has a spare winding sensor, switching to it is safer than entering a correction.
  2. Alarm threshold left unchanged: a correction and a raised threshold together would hide a real overheat twice over. The threshold is not changed while the cause is unknown or while a correction is in place.
  3. Sensor replacement planned: once the new sensor is fitted, its reading is verified against the IR thermometer at several load points.
  4. Temporary correction removed: when the new sensor is confirmed accurate, the correction comes out. That step is part of the repair — a forgotten correction will one day hide a real fault.

Standard IEC 60751 PT100 values. With a two-wire connection the cable resistance adds to the reading; measure at the terminal closest to the sensor where possible and note the cable resistance separately.

Preventive maintenance

  • On any temperature alarm, take a physical reading first. An IR thermometer costs minutes and settles whether you have a machine problem or a measurement problem.
  • Treat RTD sensors as consumables. They can drift with age. Add a resistance check to the periodic schedule rather than waiting for a nonsense reading.
  • Log temporary corrections and put an end date on them. A correction that becomes permanent will one day hide a real fault.
  • Read the trend shape, not just the value. A tidy, load-independent daily climb is almost never a real thermal problem.

Quick checklist

  • Measure the generator housing with an IR thermometer and read SCADA at the same moment. Is the gap larger than 10 °C?
  • Were power, current and speed normal at the alarm moments?
  • Is the cooling fan running, is its current normal, is the filter clean?
  • Measure the PT100 resistance and convert it with the table. Does it match the temperature you measured?
  • Are the terminal and cable connections tight and clean?
  • What do the other winding sensors in the same generator show?
  • Does the trend follow load, or climb steadily day by day?

Field note

The rule I carry out of this one: when a number and the machine's behaviour disagree, believe the machine until you have proved the number. A generator genuinely at 116 °C announces itself. This one was quiet, and the quiet was the evidence.

The part that needs discipline is the temporary correction. It is the right move to keep the turbine producing while you wait for a sensor — and exactly the kind of fix that becomes permanent if nobody writes it down. Removing it after the replacement is part of the repair, not an afterthought.
